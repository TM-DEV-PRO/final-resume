What system did you build that you're most proud of?

Answer (~60 sec)

The planning data layer at Impact Analytics. The problem: planners edit plans in a pivot grid — overrides, undo/redo, version comparisons — which looks like a transactional workload, but the serving side needs OLAP-style rollups across product, store, and week hierarchies. Conventional wisdom says you can't do both in ClickHouse because mutations are its weakness.

The insight was to change the write model instead of the engine: append-only versioned writes. Every planner override is an insert with a version stamp; reads take the latest version; deletes are tombstones; bulk reloads are atomic partition swaps. ClickHouse's mutation queue stays at zero — we turned its weakness into a non-issue — and the version history isn't overhead, it IS the feature: version diff, undo/redo, and an immutable audit trail come free.

I proved it with a proof-of-concept before the org committed, because there was real disagreement about whether ClickHouse belonged. That's the part I'm proudest of — not just the design, but converting an architectural argument into a measurable gate.

What difficult errors/bugs have you fixed?

Answer — three prepared stories

Three that taught me the most:

1. A latent column-aliasing bug at Uber. While migrating raw SQL to SQLAlchemy 2.0, I found a query where two joined tables had identical column names and results were silently mapping to the wrong attribute — financial data, wrong column, no error thrown. It had been in production for months. I fixed it, then added regression tests and raised changed-module coverage to 100% so the class of bug couldn't recur. Lesson: migrations are audits in disguise.

2. Worker-pool exhaustion at Masters India. During monthly GST filing peaks the platform would stall — blocking calls to the government portal were eating the entire worker pool exactly when clients needed us most. Short-term I shed bulk imports to an async queue; the real fix was making the pipeline asynchronous with chunked workers and idempotent retries, so retry storms couldn't double-register invoices. Lesson: alert on saturation, not just errors.

3. Malformed LLM plans at Impact Analytics. Our planner agent produced structurally invalid execution plans — referencing steps that didn't exist. Structural correctness was 0.54 on our offline eval. I wrote a deterministic validator that runs before anything executes, with one self-repair pass that re-prompts the LLM with the specific validation errors. Correctness went to 0.99. Lesson: put a deterministic contract around a probabilistic component.

4. Explaining Your Experience in Interviews

The framework (use every time)

5-step structure for any "tell me about X" question:
  1. Context — one sentence: what the product does and for whom. Never assume they know what "assortment planning" is.
  2. Problem — the measured pain before you acted (numbers make this credible: "8.5% of runs failed", "the cycle took a quarter").
  3. Your action — "I designed / I built / I drove" — first person singular for your work, "we" only for genuinely shared work.
  4. Result — the number, honestly tiered: "measured", "in an offline evaluation", or "our design target".
  5. Lesson — one sentence. This is what separates senior answers from mid-level ones.
Honesty tiers to say out loud: IA latency numbers = "committed design targets we engineer against" · 0.54→0.99 = "on our offline evaluation" · Uber/Masters India numbers = "measured". Interviewers reward precision about what's real; they punish getting caught inflating.
