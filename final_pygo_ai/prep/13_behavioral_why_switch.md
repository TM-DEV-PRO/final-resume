## Why I made each move (keep each under 45 seconds)

The pattern across all of these: each move added a new layer of ownership. Say that once, and every individual answer sounds like part of one story instead of four separate exits.

### GeeksforGeeks to Masters India (after about 15 months)

> At GeeksforGeeks I was shipping features on a consumer learning platform, and I learned a lot there. Masters India offered something I hadn't had yet: ownership of a regulated financial product and a real platform migration. I wanted to grow from delivering features to owning a service cutover and mentoring others. That's what happened. I led the PHP to FastAPI migration and mentored two engineers.

### Masters India to Uber via EPAM (after about 18 months)

> I had taken the GST platform through its migration and through scale. Uber via EPAM was a step up in engineering bar, in product reach and in systems complexity. I wanted to learn Bazel monorepo discipline (Bazel is the build system Uber uses for one giant shared codebase), Uber-scale engineering practices, and work that sits next to Finance and audit. That's where I owned the FRM scoping backend and the reconciliation migration from Google Sheets to MySQL.

### Uber via EPAM to Impact Analytics (after about 22 months)

> I wanted deeper ownership of data platforms and AI systems, not only service APIs. Impact Analytics offered a senior role building a ClickHouse analytics platform and agentic AI decision systems for retail planning. That was exactly where I wanted to go deep, and I've delivered there: a multi-tenant Go platform, planner pivots 15.5 times faster, and an AI decision engine with a CI quality gate.

**Say this carefully.** Older prep described the Impact Analytics charter as "CQRS analytics offload" and "the agentic clustering product". Don't use either phrase. The Postgres-to-ClickHouse CDC (change data capture) pipeline was built by a colleague, and the clustering copilot is verbal only.

## Why I'm exploring now (joined Impact Analytics in May 2026)

This is the hardest question on the page, because I joined recently, about five months ago as of October 2026. The answer must be honest, positive and short. Never badmouth anyone.

**The main answer:**

> I joined Impact Analytics in May 2026 and I'm proud of what I've built there. That includes the Go platform, the ClickHouse pre-aggregation layer and Ask Iris. I'm still delivering on that work. What I want next is a role where I can own a system for several years and keep building on it. As the role took shape, I realised the mix of scope and team structure wasn't quite the long, multi-year ownership I was looking for. So rather than drift, I'm being deliberate. I'm talking to a small number of teams, yours included, because I want the next move to be one I stay in.

**If they ask "Only five months?"**

> Yes, and I take that seriously, which is why I'm being selective. I'm not leaving because something went wrong. I'm moving toward a specific kind of role, and I'd rather be clear about that early than stay in a fit that isn't quite right.

**If they ask about four companies in five years:**

> Each move added a layer. At GeeksforGeeks it was product features, at Masters India a SaaS migration and mentoring, at Uber via EPAM platform work at Uber scale, and at Impact Analytics data and AI systems. My longest recent stretch was nearly two years at Uber, which shows that I stay when the scope keeps growing. That's what I'm optimising for now.

**Avoid:**
- Calling any team or manager toxic, or blaming anyone.
- Leading with compensation.
- Saying the current role "doesn't count" or leaving it off.
- Apologising as if I failed.
- Saying I've already left or "closed out" my work. My resume shows Impact Analytics as my current role. (Older prep said "I closed my open work professionally". Replace it with "I'm still delivering on my current work".)

## Leadership stories in STAR form (use the real headcounts)

Two different leadership roles. Never merge them: I **led 3** at Uber via EPAM and **mentored 2** at Masters India.

### Leading 3 engineers at Uber via EPAM (FRM)

**What this is.** FRM (Financial Risk Management) scoping is how Uber Finance decides which accounts and entities are material enough to need audit controls each quarter. I was the tech lead of an EPAM pod of three engineers, not their people manager.

- **Situation.** The FRM scoping backend needed a clean layered architecture. It also needed a migration of the reconciliation step from Google Sheets to MySQL, while the frontend team and Finance stakeholders kept changing requirements.
- **Task.** Own the service, keep the pod shipping, and raise test coverage inside Uber's Bazel build system.
- **Action.** I split ownership by layer: handlers (the API entry points), services (the business logic) and repositories (database access). I set PR conventions that came out of the backend team sync. I paired on the cutover of reconciliation version 2. I reviewed every PR early on, then stepped back as each engineer took real ownership.
- **Result.** The platform grew to 8 screens and **36 endpoints**, with over 1,100 unit tests. Reconciliation moved to MySQL, and Finance uses the tool for quarterly close. Quarterly reconciliation time dropped **70%, from 14 days to 3 days**, against a **$340M materiality threshold**. I also led the three engineers on the SQLAlchemy 2.0 ORM migration that raised module statement coverage to **100%**.

**Say this carefully.** Older prep said "30 plus endpoints". The resume says 36. If asked for a breakdown: about 31 functional routes in the scoping service, plus the collaboration-service APIs for comments and notifications, make 36. The 1,100+ test count is verbal, not on the PDF.

### Mentoring 2 engineers at Masters India

- **Situation.** Two junior engineers were new to FastAPI and asynchronous programming during the move from the monolith to microservices.
- **Task.** Get them shipping independently without breaking filing-day traffic.
- **Action.** I pair-programmed the first canary cutover with them. A canary sends a small share of real traffic to the new service first. I wrote the shared retry and idempotency helpers, so a retried request never files an invoice twice. I reviewed their PRs for input validation at service boundaries and for observability (logs and traces). Then I let them own full services.
- **Result.** Both shipped services on their own. The platform's p95 latency went **from 1.2 seconds to 300 milliseconds**, and throughput went **from 700 to 4,000 requests per minute**.

## Amazon Leadership Principles: which story to use

Amazon interviewers score answers against their Leadership Principles. Other companies use similar values under different names. This table points each value to a real example.

| Leadership Principle | Story to use |
|---|---|
| Ownership | The FRM reconciliation version 2 migration and owning its schema |
| Dive Deep | The column-aliasing bug at Uber FRM (Story 7). Also the ClickHouse update-strategy bake-off (see note below) |
| Deliver Results | Menu pipeline: onboarding from 24 hours to 2 hours and about $600K a year saved. Masters India: from 700 to 4,000 requests per minute |
| Hire and Develop the Best | Led a pod of 3 at Uber via EPAM. Mentored 2 at Masters India |
| Insist on the Highest Standards | Masters India test coverage from 35% to 82%. Over 1,100 FRM tests |
| Bias for Action | The Go data pump at Impact Analytics (see below) |

**The ClickHouse update-strategy bake-off.** In the PoC, handling 10K row updates on a roughly 29M-row table went from about 39 seconds with full-table merges to about 7 seconds with partition-scoped merges. That was MEASURED, but it came from an older resume version and is not on the current PDF. Use it only if asked for more ClickHouse depth.

**The Go data pump, for Bias for Action.** We needed to move ClickHouse rollup tables between ClickHouse Cloud clusters, but IP allowlists between the clusters blocked a direct copy. Instead of waiting on a network change, I wrote a custom Go data pump over ClickHouse's native TLS protocol. It moved data at about 344k rows per second combined, using double-buffered batches of 500k rows. Say "rows per second", not "requests per second". The largest copy (4.09B rows) was measured while in flight, so don't say the cutover finished.

Older prep mapped Bias for Action to "early exit from a short tenure rather than coasting". Don't use that. It frames the current job negatively and implies I've already left.

## Netflix-style culture one-liners

Netflix and similar culture-heavy interviews ask about judgment, candor and ownership. One sentence each, then be ready to expand.

- **Judgment.** "I prefer one source of truth over writing the same data to two databases, because dual writes drift apart silently. At Impact Analytics I also kept the LLM away from our 2.11-billion-row table and gave it prepared JSON context instead of a database tool." (The CDC pipeline at Impact Analytics was a colleague's work. Don't claim it.)
- **Candor.** "I'm precise about what my numbers mean. For example, our 80% accuracy is a CI promotion gate that a model must pass before release, not a claim that every customer sees 80% live."
- **Ownership.** "The FRM reconciliation version 2 branch was mine end to end, from design to cutover."

**Say this carefully about the 70%.** Older prep told me to call the FRM 70% cut "a design-doc target, not a measured KPI". That guidance is obsolete. The resume states 70%, from 14 days to 3 days, as the outcome. Don't walk it back unprompted. If pressed: "The technical design doc framed that cut, and that's what the platform delivered for the close."
